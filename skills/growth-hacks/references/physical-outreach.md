# An unusual physical artifact needs a useful reason

A small physical artifact can make a relevant interaction memorable: a printed workflow map, a personalized sample output, or a consumable shared by a willing team. Treat this as a proposed attention mechanism, not a proven conversion tactic. The interesting question is whether it helps a named buyer understand the job better.

Before proposing it, establish recipient relevance, whether receiving it is welcome, a suitable business delivery route, the artifact's connection to the product, and total creation/shipping/handling cost. Do not infer home addresses or spend/order anything as the agent. An explicit request for a gift plan does not authorize a purchase or delivery.

A hypothetical data-workflow product might offer a printed before/after map of a prospect's publicly described process, using synthetic data. A useful small digital specimen can test whether they want the analysis before any physical production. A random expensive gift with a sales card is a different hypothesis and may attract attention without buyer relevance.

Define the exact promise, delivery owner, maximum liability, cancellation point, and what can no longer be recalled after the human commits funds. Recheck replies/declines before a planned order. Never imply a gift is already on its way when it has not been ordered. Respect a recipient's stated preference to decline gifts.

If physical delivery would expand the current one-channel experiment, name that change for the founder to choose instead of quietly adding it. Draft the needed artifact/fulfillment brief only after the channel and budget fit the request. Keep a small comparison where practical and measure qualified conversation or useful action, not photographs of the package.

A completed delivery is an observed event, not a sale. Record failures, costs, and relevant subsequent outcomes separately. Do not reuse a vendor's claimed uplift as the founder's expected result.
