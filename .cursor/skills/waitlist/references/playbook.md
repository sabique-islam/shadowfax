# Waitlist and access-wave playbook

## Decide whether to have a queue

Use a queue when access has a real constraint. If the product works and support can handle users, an immediate trial usually teaches more than collecting addresses. A prelaunch audience can validate curiosity; it cannot demonstrate repeat value before use.

Define the promise in concrete terms: who gets access, what exists today, what remains planned, when the next update arrives, and how selections work. If there is no delivery date, say so. Never create fake queue positions, fake demand counters, or deadlines that reset.

## Minimal entry flow

Landing promise → email plus one useful qualification question → confirmation → optional share → honest status → access invitation → first value. Give subscribers an exit. A referral link should not expose an email address or provide public access to private queue data.

A useful qualification question is about the job: “Which export are you trying to import, and how often?” Job title alone may not predict need. Separate users who want the product from people collecting prizes.

## Referrals without a liability surprise

Specify counted referrals (for example, unique verified eligible people), reward tiers if any, caps, exclusions, settlement dates, and actual access-priority effect. Use both expected and maximum fulfillment cost. Do not launch a physical-reward ladder without shipping/support capacity. A free launch kit relevant to the job may beat cash for learning who cares, but that is a hypothesis.

[Harry's founder post](https://tim.blog/2014/07/21/harrys-prelaunchr-email/) reports nearly 100,000 emails in one week around a referral reward ladder. It is a historical self-report about list growth, not retained demand or a promise for another launch. Its [archived prelaunchr code](https://github.com/harrystech/prelaunchr) illustrates history; do not deploy an old stack unchanged.

## Hypothetical access-wave plan

Capacity: onboard five teams per week. Cohort: 20 qualified teams needing a weekly import. First wave: five invitations, each with an explicit seven-day claim window if the team can honor it. Track delivery, claimed access, first successful sample import, and a return for the next weekly file. Keep the other 15 classified as uninvited, not churned.

Chosen pilot signal: three of the first five teams complete an import and at least two return for a second scheduled file within 14 days. This is a planning threshold, not a benchmark. If nobody starts, interview about timing and invitation clarity. If starts fail, repair activation. If first value occurs but nobody returns, inspect recurrence before inviting all remaining teams.

## Message jobs

- Confirmation: acknowledge the request, restate access reality, and give the next update date.
- Optional share: explain why a peer might benefit and exactly what, if anything, a referral changes.
- Invitation: provide the next action, working link, support contact, and an honest expiry only if needed.
- Delay: name the change, revised update date, and easy exit. Do not manufacture an apology story.
- Nonclaim check: ask whether the need changed; stop after a useful reminder rather than infinite nudges.

Each message needs fallback text for missing names or unknown use cases. Ask the founder to approve facts such as capacity or date by supplying them; do not send messages while these remain placeholders.

## Measurement

Track entry cohort, source, verification, qualification, referral origin, invitation date, claimed access, first value, repeat value, and purchase where applicable. Deduplicate people/accounts and exclude internal tests. Report invited-to-activated separately from total-list-to-activated. Queue rank and share count are operational signals, not the success metric.
